How do you get someone ready for their first crypto purchase?

As a solo project, my goal was to help first-time crypto buyers avoid costly mistakes by practicing them first.

Who it's for, and when.

"Crypto is confusing" was too broad to design for, so I narrowed it down: who to build for, and when they need it most.

Who: first-time buyers, 18–25

They meet crypto through social posts, where hype and scams find them first, and most don't have someone experienced to ask before they buy.

When: before the first real purchase

After a mistake, the money is already gone and help comes too late. Before the first buy, people are motivated to learn and nothing is at stake yet.

Solution

Introducing CryptoSim: a practice run for your first crypto purchase.

You name what you plan to buy. CryptoSim then rehearses the moments that cost first-timers money, on real prices with practice money, and tracks habits instead of profits.

Key insights

One session isn't evidence, so I kept only what outside research also showed.

Warnings don't change behavior.

I pasted my recovery phrase seconds after a "do not share" warning. Brain-imaging research shows people tune out a warning by the second time they see it.

Anderson et al., BYU

Became: missions where you make the risky move safely and see what it would cost.

People check the ends. Scammers change the middle.

Wallets shorten addresses to the first and last few characters. 21% of US holders have sent crypto to the wrong address, and one person lost $68M to a look-alike.

Kraken, 2025 · CoinDesk, 2024

Became: Pay a friend back, which shows the full address.

Fees show up too late.

My fee appeared only after I sent, and only in ETH. One in five people who've owned crypto say fees surprised them.

SEC Investor Advocate, 2026

Became: a question about the total cost before the first buy.

Exploring

Which problems to solve, and how.

First I narrowed the problems to the ones a newcomer can practice. Then I compared four ways to solve them.

Prioritizing: which problems can a newcomer actually rehearse?

I scored each problem on how many people it hits, how much it costs, and whether something already solves it. Then I asked one more question: can you practice it before it happens?

Problem map

Scale from FBI IC3 (scams), Kraken (wrong address) and SEC Investor Advocate research (lost keys, fees). Positions are my judgment.

I cut anything that could only be explained, not practiced. That ruled out taxes and general crypto education, even though both are real problems.

Four directions, and why three lost.

I judged each against one question: does it prevent the mistake before the money moves?

ExploredTransaction explainer

AI explains any transaction in plain English.

Already exists in several explorers, and it explains after the money is gone.

Big wallets already ship this, and false reassurance from a small tool is harmful.

My assessment. CryptoSim's cost is effort: it only works if people choose to practice, which is why that's the first assumption I'm testing.

The pivot: "Make your first mistakes" wasn't the job. Getting ready for a real purchase was.

The first version was a set of missions about mistakes, and clean runs paid out practice money to spend in the market. Reviewing it, two things didn't hold up.

The rewards tainted the market.

Earning money from missions meant your portfolio no longer reflected real prices. Practice money that grows by playing well teaches the wrong lesson about crypto.

It wasn't anchored to anything.

Missions were practice in the abstract. But a first-timer has a specific purchase in mind, and that purchase is what they need to be ready for.

Before, a clean run paid $25 of practice money and a slip cost $40. After, the screen shows what a slip would cost in real life, and nothing is paid or charged.

That reframe added three things: a plan you set at the start, a Market practice track built around it, and a "market moment" when a coin you hold really drops. It also removed one: mission rewards.

Testing and iterating

Two things I changed after using it.

People could tap through the tutorial without learning anything

Testing the first run, I could finish the guided trade in seconds without reading a word. Every tip pointed at the next button, so the fastest path skipped the lesson.

The fee step

Before, the tip said "Now buy" and pointed at the button. After, it asks what the buy costs in total and only moves on after the right answer. Picking $0.20 explains the real mistake: that's only the trading fee.

I didn't slow down every step. "Tap Bitcoin" only moves you to the right screen, so tapping through it loses nothing. The question sits on the one step that teaches something.

Mistakes were explained, not shown

When you sent to the look-alike, a panel slid over the app and explained what went wrong. It read like a lesson, and it's not what a real mistake looks like. In a real wallet you'd find out from the wallet itself, and from a friend asking where their money is.

The same mistake, sending to a look-alike

Before, a panel slid over the app to explain, with boxes inside it. After, the wallet's own screen shows what happened: −40 USDC, the wrong address next to the right one with the different middle highlighted, and the friend asking where the money is.

So every mission now ends on a screen the user will actually see in a real wallet. The lesson comes from recognizing it, not from reading about it.

Constraints

Realistic enough to teach, never realistic enough to misuse.

So I had to ask: how real can practice get before it could be misused?

Real prices or a scripted market?
OptionsScripted price paths · Live market prices
ChoseLive prices, with practice money
Trade-offI can't schedule a dip. The market moment only happens when the market really moves.

Guardrails

No gambling mechanics.

No leaderboards, no "top gainers," no coin recommendations, no streak guilt. Every gain says "results reflect the market, not skill."

Nothing that works as the real thing.

No real wallet or exchange branding, every scenario labeled practice, and no realistic proof-of-payment screens that could be reused for scams.

Every number has a source.

Each stat in the app names where it comes from (SEC, FBI, Kraken, Chainalysis), and fees match what real apps charge in 2026.

Practice money only.

Nothing real is bought, sold or sent until the one real step: a send on a free test network, using test funds with no value.

Reflection

What I learned

About building product

Decide what you're scoring first.

Once I asked "does this reward a habit or luck?", cutting rewards and profit scores felt obvious, not painful.

Scope to a moment, not a topic.

"Crypto education" had no edges. "The week before your first purchase" told me what to build and what to cut.

Watch for the fastest path.

People take the quickest route through anything. If it skips the learning, the design is teaching the wrong thing.

About crypto

Custody is a decision people don't know they're making.

An exchange can reset your password. In self-custody, whoever holds the recovery phrase holds the money, and no one can reset it.

Public records cut both ways.

Anyone can see who you pay, so a scammer can plant a look-alike address in your history with a tiny payment. No hacking needed.

"Sent" and "final" are different moments.

My wallet said "Successfully sent" before anything was confirmed. Before that a transfer can still fail. After it, nothing can be undone.

None of these took a deeper understanding of blockchains. Each is a decision that arrives without warning on a screen, which makes it a product problem.

What's next

Test with first-time buyers.

Five people aged 18–25, watching where they skip, stall or get it wrong.

Check the riskiest assumption.

Does practice carry over? Compare their real test sends with their practice runs.

Cut what doesn't teach.

Whatever people skip or misread gets reworked or removed.
